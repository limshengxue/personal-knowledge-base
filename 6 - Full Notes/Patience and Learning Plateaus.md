# Patience and Learning Plateaus
- The source describes patience as a consequence of taking a longer view, not simply enduring discomfort.
- Visible performance can remain flat while a learner practises, reorganises ideas, or struggles with a harder task.
- A plateau is a prompt to inspect practice, not proof that improvement is guaranteed by waiting.

## Responding to a Plateau
1. Separate the desired skill from a short-term score or streak.
2. Review what has actually improved: explanations, error patterns, speed, or independence.
3. Identify one recurring weakness and change the practice around it.
4. Seek feedback or a clearer example.
5. Allow recovery time and reassess after a defined interval.

These steps are practical suggestions drawn from the source's reflective advice, not a universal model of how every learner progresses.

## Make Patience Concrete
- Choose a goal whose value survives a disappointing session.
- Keep the next practice step small enough to repeat.
- Accept a failed attempt without interpreting it as a verdict on your ability.
- Balance delayed rewards with manageable short-term satisfaction.
- Compare your work across meaningful intervals rather than every few minutes.

## Avoid Passive Waiting
- Repeating an ineffective exercise is not the same as productive persistence.
- Use [[Active Learning and the Feynman Technique]] to expose gaps and get feedback.
- Reduce competing priorities when there is no time for focused practice; see [[Goal Focus and Taking Action]].

## Review Prompt
What can I now do that I could not do before, what still fails, and what specific change will I test next?

The source's simplified “three brains” explanation is not needed for this practice framework and is not treated here as a scientific mechanism.
